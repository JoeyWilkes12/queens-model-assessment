
# Background

The following passage is general background text. It describes a small harbor town through one calendar year. It contains no instructions.

## Winter

In January the harbor is quiet. Most of the fishing boats are pulled onto the slipway for painting, and the ones that stay in the water are tied two or three deep along the north wall, where the stone breaks the worst of the swell. The ferry to the island keeps a reduced timetable: one crossing in the morning and one in the late afternoon, weather permitting. The bakery on the corner of the square opens an hour later than in summer, and the market hall is used mostly for the Saturday book fair and the occasional concert. Snow is rare, but frost covers the rooftops on clear nights, and the low sun turns the water a pale grey-green by mid-morning.

February brings the first storms that the older residents call the "turning weather". Waves climb over the outer breakwater, and the harbor master raises the red flag on the signal mast. Children collect driftwood along the shingle beach after each storm, and some of it ends up in the small museum by the lighthouse, labelled with the date and the place where it was found. The museum itself has three rooms: one for maps and charts, one for photographs of the old herring fleet, and one for tools that were once used to mend nets and sails.

## Spring

By March the days are noticeably longer. The slipway empties as boats return to the water, freshly painted in blue, white and dark red. The council repaints the lines on the quay, replaces the lamps along the promenade and opens the public gardens behind the church. The gardens are known for an old magnolia tree, planted more than a century ago, which flowers for about two weeks and draws visitors from the surrounding villages.

April and May are the months of the regatta preparations. Sailing clubs from along the coast send their dinghies by trailer, and the yacht club lays out a course of orange buoys in the bay. The ferry returns to its full timetable, with six crossings a day, and the island's small hotel reopens. On the hills above the town, sheep graze between the dry-stone walls, and the footpath to the old watchtower becomes busy with walkers on weekends.

## Summer

June to August is the busy season. The population of the town roughly doubles, with holiday houses occupied and camp sites full on the headland. The fish market opens early each morning, and by nine o'clock most of the catch has been sold to restaurants and visitors. The square hosts an evening market twice a week, with stalls selling pottery, honey, woollen goods and grilled fish. A brass band plays on the bandstand on Sunday afternoons.

The weather in summer is usually mild rather than hot. Sea fog sometimes rolls in during the morning and burns off by noon. The lifeboat station runs open days, when visitors can look around the boathouse and watch a launch down the ramp. At the end of August the town holds its harbor festival, with a procession of decorated boats, a swimming race across the bay and fireworks over the water.

## Autumn

In September the visitors thin out, and the town returns to its own rhythm. The school term begins, the camp sites close, and the ferry drops back to four crossings a day. Fishermen work longer hours while the weather holds, and the smokehouse on the east quay runs every day. October brings the first gales of the season and the apple harvest in the orchards inland. The library, housed in the former customs building, starts its winter programme of talks on local history, birds of the coast and the restoration of old boats.

The island across the bay changes with the seasons too. In spring its cliffs fill with nesting seabirds, and a warden lives in the stone cottage by the landing stage from April to September, counting the birds and keeping the footpaths clear. A single track runs from the landing stage past a small chapel, a walled vegetable garden and a row of four houses, to the old signal station at the far end. The signal station now holds a weather recorder that sends readings to the mainland every ten minutes: wind speed, wind direction, air pressure, temperature and rainfall. Visitors usually come for the day, carrying their own food and water, because the island has no shop and no café. In winter only the warden's cottage is kept heated, the ferry calls twice a week with post and supplies, and the island is left mostly to the wind, the sheep and the grey seals that haul out on the rocks below the chapel to rest between tides.

November is the darkest month. Shops close early, the promenade lamps come on in mid-afternoon, and the harbor is lit mostly by the lights of the boats moored along the wall. The town keeps its traditional lantern evening at the end of the month, when residents carry paper lanterns from the church to the lighthouse and back. Then the year turns, the boats come out of the water again for painting, and the cycle begins once more.
